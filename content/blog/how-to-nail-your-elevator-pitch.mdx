You step into an elevator. The person next to you is a partner at Sequoia. You have 60 seconds before the doors open. What do you say?

Most founders fumble this moment. They either ramble about features or freeze up entirely. But with the right framework, you can deliver a pitch that sticks.

## The 60-Second Framework

The best elevator pitches follow a simple four-part structure. Each part earns you the right to say the next.

### 1. The Hook (10 seconds)

Start with a problem statement that makes the listener lean in. Don't start with your company name or what you do — start with **why you exist**.

- "Did you know that 73% of investor pitches fail in the first 30 seconds?"
- "Last year, startups left $4.2B on the table because of bad pitch delivery."

<Callout type="tip">
The hook should be a single sentence. If you need two sentences, your hook isn't sharp enough.
</Callout>

### 2. The Problem (15 seconds)

Now that you have attention, frame the problem in human terms. Investors fund solutions to **painful** problems, not mild inconveniences.

Be specific. "Communication is hard" is weak. "Sales teams waste 12 hours per week writing follow-up emails that never get opened" is powerful.

### 3. The Solution (20 seconds)

This is where you introduce your product — but keep it outcome-focused. Don't explain how it works. Explain **what changes** when someone uses it.

Good: "Pitchr analyzes your pitch in real-time and gives you an investor-grade score with specific fixes."

Bad: "We use large language models and speech-to-text APIs to process audio inputs and generate rubric-based assessments."

### 4. The Ask (15 seconds)

Every great pitch ends with a clear next step. Don't leave the conversation open-ended.

- "I'd love to show you a 5-minute demo. Can I send you a calendar link?"
- "We're raising a seed round. Could I send you our deck?"

## Common Mistakes

**Trying to say everything.** The elevator pitch isn't a comprehensive overview — it's a trailer. Leave them wanting more.

**No emotional resonance.** Numbers matter, but stories stick. One specific customer anecdote beats ten statistics.

**Forgetting the ask.** A pitch without an ask is just a monologue.

## Practice Makes Permanent

The difference between a good pitcher and a great one isn't talent — it's **reps**. Record yourself delivering your elevator pitch five times. You'll be shocked how different take one sounds from take five.

The founders who raise the most aren't necessarily building the best products. They're the ones who've practiced telling the story until it's second nature.

Your pitch is worth practicing. Your startup depends on it.
